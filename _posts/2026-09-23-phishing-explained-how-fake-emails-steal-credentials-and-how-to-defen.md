A thief doesn’t smash your car door; they study how you unlock it, then hand you a fake key. In the digital world, phishing is the same trick: attackers exploit your habits and trust instead of breaking security code. They send an email that looks like your bank, a text that mimics a delivery service, or a chat message pretending to be a colleague. The message contains a link or attachment that, when clicked, steals your login details – the password – or installs malware – malicious software that hijacks your computer. Because the request feels familiar, you often comply without thinking. This works because people trust familiar brands and urgent language, just like you’d trust a familiar driver’s voice to open your car. The defense is simple: treat any unexpected request for personal info as suspicious, verify the sender through a separate channel, and never click links directly from messages you didn’t expect. By recognizing the pattern of trust exploitation, you can stop phishing before it reaches your credentials. You now understand that phishing attacks succeed by mimicking trusted communication, not by cracking technical defenses.






Can you recall a time you almost clicked a suspicious link because it seemed to come from a trusted source?

## Fake Keys: Phishing Over Car Locks

Attackers target trust, not technical barriers.

## Mimic Trusted Brands

Emails look like banks, deliveries, or coworkers.

## What They Steal

Login credentials or malware installed via deceptive links.

## Where It Breaks

When you verify the sender or avoid unexpected clicks.

## Why It Matters

One compromised password can give attackers full account control.
