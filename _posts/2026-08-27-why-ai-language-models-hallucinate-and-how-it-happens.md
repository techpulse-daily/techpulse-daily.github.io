Imagine a student forced to answer every multiple‑choice question, even when they don’t know the answer. That pressure mirrors how large language models (LLMs — AI systems that predict text) generate output. An LLM tries to fill in the next word based on patterns it saw during training, just like the student guesses to avoid a blank. When the model lacks a solid fact, it stitches together plausible‑sounding fragments, creating a hallucination — an answer that sounds correct but isn’t rooted in reality. This happens because the model’s objective is to minimize prediction error, not to verify truth. Training data gaps act like missing study notes, and the model fills the void with the most statistically likely phrase. The result is a confident but invented statement, similar to a student’s wild guess that still looks right on the page. Understanding that hallucinations stem from pattern‑driven prediction rather than factual lookup lets you anticipate when an AI might “make stuff up” and plan safeguards.
