Telling your boss about a missed deadline on Friday morning is an emergency, but telling them on Monday is just a helpful heads‑up.

## Managing up means zero surprises

Managing up is the deliberate practice of keeping your leadership informed before problems turn into fires. The idea is simple: if you surface a blocker the moment it appears, you give the people who schedule, allocate resources, and set expectations a chance to react while the cost of change is still low. In the script we see a database query that slows down a migration. The instinct for many engineers is to stay quiet, to fix it alone, and to avoid “bothering” the manager. That instinct leads to a twenty‑hour sprint wrestling with an index, all while the rest of the team proceeds under the assumption that the migration will finish on schedule.

## Silence creates emergencies

When the engineer finally surfaces the delay, the director is forced to treat it as an emergency. The release schedule is already locked, downstream testing is booked, and stakeholders have been briefed. Because the information arrived late, the director cannot adjust the timeline without causing ripple effects: test teams must be re‑scheduled, customers may see a later feature rollout, and the organization’s credibility takes a hit. The emergency is not the technical problem itself; it is the surprise that prevented proactive mitigation.

## Early notes save timelines

If, instead, the engineer had sent a brief note the moment the query slowdown was observed, the timeline could have been shifted while adjustments were still cheap. A simple “I’ve hit a blocker on the migration; the index is taking longer than expected. I estimate an additional 20 hours” gives the manager enough information to reprioritize, communicate to other teams, or allocate additional resources. The cost of the shift is minimal compared to the cost of scrambling after the fact.

## Share the bad news early

The hardest part of this practice is habit. Engineers often equate silence with competence, assuming that fixing a problem alone demonstrates ownership. In reality, ownership includes keeping the decision‑makers informed so they can make better trade‑offs. The script ends with a direct prompt: *What is the hardest project status you are currently holding back from your manager right now?* The answer to that question determines whether you are managing up effectively or setting the stage for an avoidable emergency.

By treating every emerging issue as a piece of information that belongs on the manager’s radar, you eliminate surprises, preserve schedule slack, and keep the organization’s larger objectives in view. The practice is not about escalating every minor hiccup; it is about recognizing the threshold where a delay begins to impact downstream plans and communicating at that point. In engineering terms, it is the difference between a controlled variance and an uncontrolled incident. The discipline of “zero surprises” therefore becomes a core part of reliable delivery.
